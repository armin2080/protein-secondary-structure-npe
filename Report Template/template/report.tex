\section{Introduction}

Proteins are linear chains of amino acids that fold into complex three-dimensional structures
determining their biological function. An intermediate level of organization is the
\textit{secondary structure}, where each amino acid residue participates in local folding
patterns: $\alpha$-helices, $\beta$-sheets, or random coils \parencite{bioninja}.
Predicting secondary structure from sequence alone is a fundamental problem in computational
biology.

In this project, we formulate secondary structure prediction as a probabilistic inference
problem. We define a two-state Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with fixed transition and emission
probabilities derived from empirical protein data \parencite{kaggle}. The HMM's hidden states
represent $\alpha$-helix versus ``other'' (encompassing $\beta$-sheets and coils). Given an
amino acid sequence, the Forward-Backward algorithm \parencite{jurafsky} computes the exact
per-position posterior probability of each state — this serves as our ground truth.

We then train a neural posterior estimator using \textit{BayesFlow} \parencite{bayesflow}
to approximate these posteriors from amino acid sequences alone, without access to the HMM
parameters. We compare two training designs for the summary network that conditions
BayesFlow: a staged design that pretrains it separately and freezes it, and a joint,
end-to-end design that lets BayesFlow's own training signal shape it directly
(Section~\ref{sec:joint}). The trained models are validated on held-out simulated sequences
and on a real protein: human insulin (PDB: 1A7F) \parencite{insulin}.

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the overall workflow: an HMM simulator produces
synthetic sequences with exact Forward--Backward posteriors, a BiLSTM--CouplingFlow
architecture amortises this posterior as a function of the sequence alone, and the trained
model is validated against the experimental secondary-structure annotation of a real
protein.

\begin{figure}[ht]

    \caption{Illustration of the pipeline. \emph{Simulation}: the two-state HMM
    generates synthetic sequences with exact $P(\alpha)$ targets via Forward--Backward.
    \emph{Amortized inference}: a BiLSTM summary network plus a CouplingFlow learn to

\end{figure}

The rest of the report covers the data (Section~2), the HMM model (Section~3),
the approximator (Section~4), training (Section~5), diagnostics (Section~6),
inference results (Section~7), and conclusions (Section~8).


\section{Data}

The data layer has two purposes. First, it produces an unlimited supply of simulated amino-acid sequences combined with exact per-residue inference targets. Second, it converts a real protein structure, human insulin 1A7F, into the same sequence representation and a compatible two-state validation target.

\subsection{Canonical representation}

Each amino acid is represented by an integer in {0, . . . , 19} in the canonical
order
\begin{equation}
(\mathrm{A,R,N,D,C,E,Q,G,H,I,L,K,M,F,P,S,T,W,Y,V}).
\end{equation}
For example, alanine is encoded as 0, glycine as 7, and valine as 19. The mapping,
inverse mapping, state labels, and all probability arrays are defined once in
\texttt{encoding.py}; the hidden-state convention is $0=\alpha$-helix and
$1=\text{other}$, and probability arrays are checked at import time to sum to
one. Using the same representation for simulation and insulin parsing ensures
the validation input is compatible with the training data; the full alphabet size
is always 20, even for short sequences.

\subsection{Simulated training data}

The simulator draws a state path $s_{1:L}$ from the two-state HMM of
Section~\ref{sec:stat}, then emits one amino acid per position from the
state's emission table, yielding the aligned sequences $s_{1:L}$ and
$x_{1:L}$.

All randomness is supplied through a NumPy \texttt{Generator}. Consequently, a fixed
seed reproduces sequence lengths, state paths, and emitted amino acids exactly. For a
batch of $n$ examples, lengths are sampled independently from the discrete uniform
distribution
\begin{equation}
L_i \sim \mathcal{U}\{10,11,\ldots,60\}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
\end{equation}
This range covers both short insulin chains while retaining longer sequences that show
several state transitions. The training script can request as many examples as required;
in the final configuration, 2000 training and 400 validation sequences were generated,
with independent held-out sequences for evaluation.

The sampled state path is retained for diagnostics of the generative process, even
though the neural target is the Forward-Backward posterior.

\subsection{Exact targets and dataset handoff}

For every simulated observation $x_{1:L}$, exact HMM inference produces
\begin{equation}
y_t = P(S_t=0\mid X_{1:L}=x_{1:L}), \qquad t=1,\ldots,L,
\end{equation}
where state 0 denotes $\alpha$-helix. Thus one training item consists of the integer
sequence, its per-position $\alpha$ posterior, and the sampled state path. The target is
a probability in $[0,1]$, not a hard classification label.

Since sequences have variable length, storing every example in a single padded matrix would introduce unnecessary padding. Instead, sequences are concatenated into a one-dimensional array and accompanied by a vector of sequence lengths.

A saved .npz file contains sequences
(integer observations), posteriors (floating-point targets), states (integer latent paths),
and lengths. Cumulative sums of lengths allows exact reconstruction of the example sequence. The model doesn’t need object arrays and pickling; it is portable and allows the system to know how to pad and mask itself. Table~\ref{tab:data-interfaces} shows the main interfaces that are exposed to the rest of the system.

\section{Statistical model}
\label{sec:stat}

\subsection{Two-state Hidden Markov Model}

Define $S_t\in\{0,1\}$ to be the state of the secondary structure at position $t$, where $S_t=0$ represents an $\alpha$-helix and $S_t=1$ represents “other”. The other state includes the $\beta$-sheet and random coil secondary structures. Let $X_t\in\{0,\ldots,19\}$ represent the amino acid at that position. The assumptions underlying the model are that the process is a first-order Markov process and that observations are conditionally independent given the current state:

\begin{equation}
P(s_{1:L},x_{1:L})
= P(s_1)P(x_1\mid s_1)
  \prod_{t=2}^{L} P(s_t\mid s_{t-1})P(x_t\mid s_t).
\label{eq:hmm-joint}
\end{equation}

The initial distribution is deterministic:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\pi}
=\begin{bmatrix}P(S_1=0)&P(S_1=1)\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:start}
\end{equation}
Every simulated sequence therefore starts in the other state. State transitions follow
the row-stochastic matrix
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}=
\begin{bmatrix}
P(0\mid0)&P(1\mid0)\\
P(0\mid1)&P(1\mid1)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.90&0.10\\
0.05&0.95
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:transition}
\end{equation}
Thus an $\alpha$-helix continues with probability 0.90, while an other segment continues
with probability 0.95. The expected run lengths are $1/(1-0.90)=10$ residues for the
$\alpha$ state and $1/(1-0.95)=20$ residues for the other state. Solving
$\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathbf{A}$ gives the stationary distribution
$\boldsymbol{\mu}=(1/3,2/3)$. The deterministic start distribution differs from this
stationary distribution, so short sequences are initially more strongly biased toward
the other state.

The state-conditional emission probabilities are fixed to the empirical values in
Table~\ref{tab:emissions}. Each row is a categorical distribution over the canonical
amino-acid order and sums to one.

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{Emission probabilities (\%) for the two HMM states.}
    \label{tab:emissions}
    \footnotesize
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.4pt}
    \begin{tabular}{l|rrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrr}
        \hline
        State & A & R & N & D & C & E & Q & G & H & I & L & K & M & F & P & S & T & W & Y & V \\
        \hline
        $\alpha$ & 12 & 6 & 3 & 5 & 1 & 9 & 5 & 4 & 2 & 7 & 12 & 6 & 3 & 4 & 2 & 5 & 4 & 1 & 3 & 6 \\
        other & 6 & 5 & 5 & 6 & 2 & 5 & 3 & 9 & 3 & 5 & 8 & 6 & 2 & 4 & 6 & 7 & 6 & 1 & 4 & 7 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The emission table is intentionally simple. For example, alanine and leucine are more
probable under the $\alpha$ state, while glycine and proline are more probable under the
other state. However, the distributions overlap substantially; individual residues are
not decisive, and inference must combine evidence across the full sequence with the
persistence encoded in $\mathbf{A}$.

\subsection{Generative process}

Sequence of length $L$ was generated through the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Set $S_1=1$ according to the initial distribution in
    Equation~\ref{eq:start}.
    \item For $t=2,\ldots,L$, draw $S_t\sim\operatorname{Categorical}
    (\mathbf{A}_{S_{t-1},:})$.
    \item At every position, draw $X_t\sim\operatorname{Categorical}
    (\mathbf{B}_{S_t,:})$, where $\mathbf{B}$ is the emission matrix in
    Table~\ref{tab:emissions} divided by 100.
\end{enumerate}
The process produces both the observation $x_{1:L}$ and its latent path $s_{1:L}$.
The neural approximator does not receive the path. Instead, it is trained on the exact
posterior marginals computed from the observation alone.

\subsection{Forward-Backward inference}

Because all HMM parameters are known, the posterior probability of each state can be
computed exactly with the Forward-Backward algorithm \parencite{jurafsky}. Define the
forward quantity
\begin{equation}
f_t(j)=P(X_{1:t}=x_{1:t},S_t=j)
\end{equation}
and the backward quantity
\begin{equation}
b_t(i)=P(X_{t+1:L}=x_{t+1:L}\mid S_t=i).
\end{equation}
With emission probability $B_{j,x_t}=P(X_t=x_t\mid S_t=j)$, their recursions are
\begin{align}
f_1(j) &= \pi_j B_{j,x_1}, \\
f_t(j) &= B_{j,x_t}\sum_{i=0}^{1} f_{t-1}(i)A_{ij},
          &&t=2,\ldots,L, \\
b_L(i) &= 1, \\
b_t(i) &= \sum_{j=0}^{1}A_{ij}B_{j,x_{t+1}}b_{t+1}(j),
          &&t=L-1,\ldots,1.
\end{align}
The smoothed posterior marginal is then
\begin{equation}
\gamma_t(j)
=P(S_t=j\mid x_{1:L})
=\frac{f_t(j)b_t(j)}{\sum_{k=0}^{1}f_t(k)b_t(k)}.
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
The training target is the $\alpha$ column, $y_t=\gamma_t(0)$.

This implementation uses \texttt{hmmlearn.CategoricalHMM} directly \parencite{hmmlearn}. The arrays \texttt{startprob\_}, \texttt{transmat\_}, and \texttt{emissionprob\_} are taken from the common constants, while the alphabet size is fixed at 20. No fitting is done: invoking \texttt{.fit()} function would change the parameters and no longer would correspond to the intended HMM. In a batch, variable length sequences are concatenated into a column vector and given along with their respective lengths. \texttt{predict\_proba} performs the inference independently for each sequence and provides both posterior columns, keeping column 0 as a target.

\subsection{Independent correctness check}

A two-residue observation checks the transition orientation and emission
indexing. For $x=(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{A})$ (indices $(7,0)$), the
Forward--Backward recursions give $f_2(0)=0.09\times0.05\times0.12=0.00054$
and $f_2(1)=0.09\times0.95\times0.06=0.00513$, hence
$P(S_2{=}0\mid\mathrm{G,A})=\frac{0.00054}{0.00054+0.00513}=0.0952$, while
$P(S_1{=}0\mid\mathrm{G,A})=0$ because of the deterministic start. The library
output matches this result and an independent NumPy implementation, and six
additional length-two and length-three sequences match as well.

\subsection{Verification and reproducibility}

The simulator was evaluated on 2000 independently generated sequences of length
500 (seed 12345): the maximum empirical deviation from the specified transition
and emission probabilities was 0.0004 and 0.0007, far below the Monte Carlo
threshold of 0.01. Forward--Backward verification on 200 sequences confirmed
posteriors in $[0,1]$, state probabilities summing to one, and exactly zero
$\alpha$ posterior at position 0. Same-seed generation and save/load round-trips
reproduced all arrays exactly; the automated test suite (Section~2--3) all passes.

\subsection{Interpretation and limitations}

Given fixed HMM parameters, (\ref{eq:gamma}) is a deterministic function of the
sequence. The neural network therefore amortizes the exact Forward--Backward
computation rather than learning a parameter posterior -- this motivates the
calibration checks in later sections. The HMM is intentionally simple: the
$\beta$-sheets and coils are collapsed into one state, emissions are not
position-dependent, and parameters are constant. The deterministic start state
guarantees an exact-zero $\alpha$ posterior at position 0, and the insulin
validation uses experimental hard labels rather than soft HMM posteriors.

\section{Approximator}
\label{sec:approx}

\subsection{Architecture overview}

We use BayesFlow \parencite{bayesflow} to train a neural posterior estimator. The
architecture consists of two components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Summary network:} A bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) that processes the
    variable-length amino acid sequence and produces a fixed-size summary vector.
    We use a BiLSTM rather than a permutation-invariant network because amino acid
    sequences have a meaningful sequential order — the position of each residue
    matters for secondary structure prediction.
    \item \textbf{Inference network:} A \texttt{CouplingFlow} normalizing flow
    \parencite{couplingflow} that learns the conditional density of the per-position
    $\alpha$-helix posteriors given the sequence summary.
\end{enumerate}

The BiLSTM architecture is
\begin{multline*}
\text{Embedding}(21\to 32) \to \text{BiLSTM}(\dim=64,\ \text{2 layers},\ \text{dropout }0.2) \\
\to \text{masked mean pooling} \to \text{Linear+ReLU} \to \text{summary}(\dim=64).
\end{multline*}
A separate linear \texttt{position\_head} produces per-position logits for
direct point prediction of $P(\alpha)$. Padding token 20 is used for
variable-length sequences, with \texttt{pack\_\allowbreak padded\_\allowbreak sequence}
so the LSTM skips padded positions; the masked binary cross-entropy loss
averages only over real positions. The CouplingFlow is built from affine
coupling transforms with actnorm, with two-layer hidden subnets of width
$128$; its depth is selected by hyperparameter search
(Section~\ref{sec:training}).

\subsection{Two-stage design}

Training proceeds in two stages, reflecting the fact that the summary network is not
differentiated through BayesFlow's flow loss:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 — BiLSTM pretraining:} The summary network is trained
    directly on the masked BCE objective against Forward-Backward $\alpha$-helix
    posteriors, with an auxiliary loss predicting each sequence's mean $\alpha$-helix
    fraction. This ensures both the \texttt{position\_head} and \texttt{summary\_head}
    are well-trained.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 — CouplingFlow training:} The BiLSTM is frozen. For each
    sequence, we compute a fixed-size condition vector by concatenating the pooled
    summary (dim~64) with the per-position logits (padded to \texttt{max\_len}=60,
    dim~60). The CouplingFlow (hidden widths~128, affine transform, actnorm; depth
    chosen by the search described in Section~\ref{sec:training})
    is then trained via \texttt{BasicWorkflow.fit\_offline} on
    $\{\text{condition} \to P(\alpha \mid \text{sequence})\}$.
\end{enumerate}

Raw logits (not sigmoid probabilities) are used in the condition because the
CouplingFlow's base distribution is an unconstrained Gaussian, and a second $[0,1]$-bounded
signal would destabilize training.

\subsection{Joint (end-to-end) alternative}
\label{sec:joint}

We also trained the alternative the two-stage design avoids: letting gradients
from the CouplingFlow's loss reach the BiLSTM directly, with no freezing step.
This works because BayesFlow runs on Keras 3's \texttt{torch} backend, where a
Keras layer is a \texttt{torch.nn.Module}; wrapping the same BiLSTM in
\texttt{keras.layers.TorchModuleWrapper} lets \texttt{BasicWorkflow} call it as
a real summary network every step, instead of consuming a precomputed
condition. Everything else is identical (dataset, BiLSTM architecture,
CouplingFlow, model-selection criterion), and results are reported alongside
the frozen design throughout Sections~\ref{sec:training}--\ref{sec:inference}.


\section{Training and Diagnostics}
\label{sec:training}

\subsection{Two-stage design and data regime}

Training is offline: a fixed set of 2000 training and 400
validation sequences (length $10$--$60$, padded to $60$) is generated once
and reused across every epoch. The pipeline runs in two stages: first the
summary network (Section~\ref{sec:approx}) is pretrained to approximate the
Forward--Backward posteriors position by position, then it is frozen and
reused purely as a feature extractor to condition the coupling flow.
Splitting the problem this way keeps each optimisation target well defined,
and all losses and metrics are computed on real positions only; padding is
excluded throughout.

\subsection{Stage 1: summary-network pretraining}

The network minimises a masked binary cross-entropy against the soft
Forward--Backward targets, using AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight
decay $10^{-4}$), batch size $64$, gradient clipping at norm $1.0$ and a
\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} schedule, training for up to $60$ epochs with
early stopping (patience $8$). Its output forms a $124$-dimensional
condition: a $64$-dimensional pooled summary concatenated with the $60$
per-position logits. Both halves turn out to matter: an earlier version
conditioned the flow on the pooled summary alone, which discarded
positional information and performed worse than a sequence-blind baseline;
concatenating the per-position logits back in fixed that.

\subsection{Stage 2: coupling-flow training and model selection}

With the summary network frozen, the flow is trained with AdamW (learning
rate $5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$), batch size $64$, for $100$
epochs. One subtlety: the flow's objective is a negative log-density, hence
negative-valued and unbounded below, and roughly $44\%$ of every padded
target is exactly zero (padding, plus the deterministic state at
position~$0$). A continuous flow can place arbitrarily high density on a
single point, pushing this loss toward $-\infty$; selecting by it would
reward whichever config overfits the padding hardest. We therefore rank
configurations by masked mean absolute error (MAE) against the
Forward--Backward posteriors on real positions. A search over optimiser,
learning rate, depth, and weight decay (six configurations) selected a
coupling-flow depth of $10$ (Figure~\ref{fig:training}).

\subsection{Convergence}

The Stage-1 loss plateaus around $0.46$ (Figure~\ref{fig:training}) instead
of going to zero, which is not stalling: for soft probability targets,
cross-entropy is bounded below by the mean entropy of those targets. On the
validation set that floor is $0.4621$ and the network reaches $0.4623$ --- a
gap of only $0.0003$, so the flat curve sits at its theoretical minimum.
Training and validation losses track each other closely throughout, with no
sign of overfitting.

\subsection{Calibration and coverage}

Because the Forward--Backward posterior is a deterministic function of the
sequence, there is no classical parameter uncertainty here; what the flow's
spread reflects is uncertainty from the lossy summary condition. On a held-out
set of 200 test sequences (6764 real positions) the rank histogram is close to
uniform (mean rank $0.528$, Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance $0.110$) and $z$-scores
have mean $0.13$ and standard deviation $0.71$, with $96.1\%$ empirical coverage
at the nominal $90\%$ level --- slightly conservative, the safe direction.
Section~\ref{sec:diag} details these checks and the boundary effect behind the
remaining deviation.

\subsection{Joint training: configuration and cost}

The joint model reuses the same BiLSTM and CouplingFlow architectures, but
\texttt{fit\_\allowbreak offline} manages a single optimizer for the whole
model (one AdamW-with-cosine-warmup schedule), not the staged design's two
independently-tuned ones. The hyperparameter search was trimmed to four
configurations, since joint training pays the BiLSTM's forward/backward
cost on every step of every trial rather than once (Stage~1 amortises it
for the staged search). The search selected learning rate $10^{-3}$,
weight decay $10^{-3}$, depth~$6$. On this (CPU-only) machine the final
$100$-epoch retrain took $33.5$ minutes, versus $\sim17$ minutes for the
whole $4$-configuration search.

\section{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diag}

All diagnostics below are computed on a held-out set of $200$ simulated
sequences ($6{,}764$ real residue positions), independent of training and
validation. The two training designs of Section~\ref{sec:training} ---
frozen (staged) and joint (end-to-end) --- are evaluated with identical
procedures and reported side by side. Because the Forward--Backward
posterior is a deterministic function of the sequence, there is no
classical parameter uncertainty here; what these checks assess is the
uncertainty that arises from the lossy summary condition, i.e.\ whether
the flow's spread is an honest description of its own residual error.

\subsection{Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC)}

Standard SBC \parencite{sbc} draws parameters from the prior, simulates data, and checks
whether posterior ranks are uniformly distributed. Our setting differs: the HMM parameters
are fixed (not inferred), and the Forward--Backward target is a deterministic function of
the input. We adapt SBC by computing, for each sequence position, the rank of the true
Forward--Backward posterior value within the BayesFlow posterior samples, and testing
uniformity of these ranks. Under perfect learning the true value is itself one draw from
the learned posterior, so its rank among the $200$ posterior samples must be uniform on
$\{0,1,\ldots,200\}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:sbc} shows the rank histogram and the PIT ECDF for both designs. The frozen
design achieves a mean rank of $0.528$ ($44.2\%$ of ranks below $0.5$) and a
Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) distance of $0.110$; the joint design achieves a mean rank of
$0.519$ ($45.0\%$ below $0.5$) and KS $=0.132$. Both are close to uniform, with two
remaining deviations identical in kind across designs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spike in the first rank bin.} Roughly $3\%$ of test
    positions are exact-zero targets (the deterministic start state at
    position~$0$ of every sequence). A continuous normalizing flow cannot
    represent a point mass: it spreads a tiny positive density around zero,
    so all $200$ samples exceed the true value and the rank is forced to
    exactly $0$. This is a representational artefact, not a calibration failure.
    \item \textbf{Mild central hump.} Ranks cluster slightly toward the
    centre --- the signature of a mildly conservative (overdispersed)
    posterior that hedges rather than risks being overconfident.
\end{itemize}

Neither design is U-shaped (no overconfidence) and neither shows a strong
directional bias. The joint design fits the Forward--Backward posterior
markedly better point-wise (Section~\ref{sec:inference}) at a small price
in rank uniformity (KS $0.132$ vs.\ $0.110$), reflecting slightly narrower
posteriors at some positions.

    designs. Each panel shows the rank histogram (left, dashed line = uniform

\subsection{Z-score analysis and credible interval coverage}

Per-position $z$-scores are computed as
$z_t = (P_t^{\mathrm{true}} - \hat{\mu}_t)/\hat{\sigma}_t$, where
$\hat{\mu}_t$ and $\hat{\sigma}_t$ are the posterior mean and standard
deviation of the BayesFlow samples at position $t$. For a well-calibrated
posterior the $z$-scores should approximately follow $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$:
mean $0$ (unbiased) and standard deviation $1$ (honest spread).

Figure~\ref{fig:zscore} reports the results after excluding the exact-zero
point-mass positions (200 of $6{,}764$). In the frozen design a small number of
positions produce extreme $z$-scores ($63$ of $6{,}564$, about $1\%;$ maximum
$|z| \approx 3137$) and are removed for display. These outliers cluster at the
\textbf{C-terminus} of sequences ($83\%$ have normalized position $\geq 0.85$):
at the last few residues the flow's posterior mean collapses toward $0$ even
when the truth is high ($P(\alpha) \in [0.3, 0.8]$). The retained $z$-scores
have mean $0.129$ and standard deviation $0.709$. The joint design is markedly
cleaner: only $18$ such positions remain (median normalized position $0.83$),
the maximum $|z|$ is $10.2$, and the $z$-scores have mean $0.105$ and standard
deviation $0.761$ --- end-to-end training mitigates the C-terminal boundary
effect.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/zscore_distribution.png}\hfill

In both designs the spread of the $z$-scores is below $1$ (frozen
$0.709$, joint $0.761$): the truth typically falls closer to the posterior
mean than the posterior's own spread would predict --- the signature of a
slightly overdispersed (conservative) posterior. This is fully consistent
with the credible intervals \textbf{over-covering} at every nominal level
(Table~\ref{tab:coverage} and lower panels of Figure~\ref{fig:zscore}). At
a nominal $90\%$ level the frozen design reaches $96.1\%$ and the joint
design $96.6\%$ coverage. Over-coverage is the safe direction --- the error
bars are honest, slightly wider than necessary --- and the excess is small
and consistent with the
central hump in the rank histograms.

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

\subsection{Synthetic held-out evaluation}

Table~\ref{tab:synthetic} reports performance on the $200$ held-out
simulated sequences ($6{,}764$ residue positions), using the exact
Forward--Backward posterior as ground truth. For BayesFlow the point
estimate is the posterior median; for the BiLSTM it is the direct sigmoid
output.

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{Performance on held-out simulated data (frozen vs.\ joint design).}
    \label{tab:synthetic}
    \begin{tabular}{l|c|c|c}
        \hline
        Model & Design & MAE & $r$ \\
        \hline
        BayesFlow (median) & Frozen & 0.057 & 0.778 \\
        BayesFlow (median) & Joint & \textbf{0.016} & \textbf{0.987} \\
        BiLSTM (point) & Frozen & \textbf{0.0035} & \textbf{0.9997} \\
        BiLSTM (point) & Joint & 0.300 & $-0.535$ \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The frozen design's BiLSTM recovers the Forward--Backward posteriors almost
perfectly ($r = 0.9997$, MAE $0.0035$), confirming that a direct network can
learn the deterministic input--output mapping of the HMM with high
fidelity. End-to-end training improves the BayesFlow posterior markedly:
its median reaches MAE $0.016$ and $r = 0.987$, versus $0.057$ and $0.778$
for the frozen design. Allowing the flow's loss to shape the summary
network lets the encoder adapt to what the flow needs, rather than to a
separate point-prediction objective.

The joint BiLSTM on its own is a poor point predictor ($r = -0.535$). This
is expected and not a defect: in the joint design the BiLSTM is never
trained on the supervised BCE objective and functions purely as a feature
extractor, so its per-position outputs should not be read as probabilities.
The BiLSTM point baseline is therefore meaningful only in the frozen
design.

Chain B ($34.5\%$ true helix) is predicted well by both designs, with
correlations of $0.85$ that track the experimental annotation closely.
Chain B's helix fraction is close to the HMM stationary distribution
($\pi \approx 33\%$), so the prior does not conflict with the evidence.

Chain A ($61.9\%$ true helix) is poorly predicted by both designs
($r \approx 0.4$). The HMM's stationary prior of about $33\%$ helix biases
predictions toward \emph{other}, and the emission tables do not carry
enough information to override that prior over only 21 residues. Running
Forward--Backward \emph{directly} on the insulin sequence yields a
similarly low correlation on chain~A ($r \approx 0.40$ against the
experimental labels), confirming that the limitation is intrinsic to the
two-state HMM rather than to the neural approximator.

The most informative comparison is across designs on insulin. Joint
training recovers the Forward--Backward posterior almost perfectly on
synthetic data ($r = 0.987$), yet the insulin correlations are essentially
unchanged (chain~A $0.391$ vs.\ $0.428$; chain~B $0.854$ vs.\ $0.851$).
Even a near-perfect posterior estimator cannot exceed what the simulator's
assumptions allow on real proteins: the inference engine is not the
bottleneck. This is direct empirical evidence for the \emph{model ceiling}
interpretation of chain~A --- the gap between the two chains quantifies how
much the HMM's structural assumptions matter for real proteins, and no
amount of training on the flow side can close it.


\section{Discussion \& Conclusion}

\subsection{What BayesFlow contributes}

In the frozen design the BiLSTM point predictor is more accurate on synthetic
data than the BayesFlow posterior median ($r = 0.9997$ vs.\ $0.778$). This is
expected: the Forward--Backward target is a deterministic function of the input
under fixed HMM parameters, so there is no genuine parameter uncertainty to
recover, and a direct network learns this map more efficiently than a flow.
Joint training lets the summary network adapt to the flow's objective, and the
resulting posterior median reaches $r = 0.987$, nearly closing that gap.

BayesFlow nevertheless provides what a point predictor cannot: posterior
samples with credible intervals (Figure~\ref{fig:contraction}), calibration
diagnostics that show the uncertainty is trustworthy (both designs over-cover at
every nominal level, Table~\ref{tab:coverage}), and an amortized framework that
generalizes directly to settings with genuine parameter uncertainty.

The insulin results are the strongest argument for the pipeline. Even though
joint training recovers the Forward--Backward posterior almost perfectly on
synthetic data, the real-protein correlations are unchanged (chain~A $\approx
0.4$, chain~B $\approx 0.85$). The neural posterior estimator is therefore not
the limiting factor --- the two-state HMM is.

\subsection{Model limitations}

The two-state HMM with fixed empirical emission tables is an oversimplified model of
protein secondary structure. The stationary $\alpha$-helix prior of $\tfrac{1}{3}$
systematically biases predictions, and the emission tables are not informative enough
to reliably overcome this prior on short sequences. This is most evident on insulin
chain~A, where the model fails regardless of whether inference is performed exactly
(Forward-Backward) or approximately (BayesFlow/BiLSTM).

\subsection{Potential improvements}

The most impactful improvement would be a richer simulator: adding a separate
$\beta$-sheet state, position-dependent emissions, or a context-aware state
process would give a more realistic prior and is expected to close part of the
gap on chain~A. The summary network could also benefit from a transformer
encoder for longer sequence context. Online simulation (fresh sequences every
batch) would eliminate any overfitting to the fixed offline dataset and could
be combined with the joint design. Finally, validating on a panel of diverse
proteins instead of a single insulin structure would turn the
chain~A/chain~B contrast into a systematic assessment.

\subsection{Reflection on own learnings}

This project made the central idea of simulation-based inference concrete: a
network can be trained to answer inference questions about a simulator without
touching its internals, and the price is paid in calibration rather than in fit.
The staged-vs.-joint comparison showed that training a summary network is a
modelling choice with measurable consequences, and that a point-estimate
baseline and a posterior estimator answer different questions. Implementing the
diagnostics by hand forced us to understand what ``calibrated'' means and how to
read failure modes from a rank histogram. Reproducibility --- seeded simulation,
versioned checkpoints, and automated checks --- made every number in this report
auditable. The insulin result was the most instructive: a perfectly trained
inference engine cannot outperform its simulator, and honest diagnostics are the
clearest way to expose that ceiling.

\subsection{Conclusion}

We successfully implemented a full simulation-based inference pipeline for protein
secondary structure: HMM simulation, Forward-Backward ground-truth generation, BiLSTM
summary network design, BayesFlow CouplingFlow training, and comprehensive calibration
diagnostics including adapted SBC, z-score analysis, and credible interval coverage.
Validation on both synthetic and real data reveals both the strengths of the neural
approximator (faithful learning of the training target, well-behaved uncertainty on
chain~B) and the fundamental limitations of the two-state HMM as a generative model of
real protein structure.

All code, check scripts, and diagnostic outputs are available in the project repository.
