\section{Introduction}

Proteins are linear chains of amino acids that fold into three-dimensional structures
determining their biological function. An intermediate level of organization is the
\textit{secondary structure}, where each residue participates in local folding
patterns: $\alpha$-helices, $\beta$-sheets, or random coils \parencite{bioninja}.
Predicting secondary structure from sequence alone is a fundamental problem in
computational biology.

In this project, we formulate secondary structure prediction as a probabilistic inference
problem. We define a two-state Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with fixed transition and emission
probabilities from empirical protein data \parencite{kaggle}, whose hidden states represent
$\alpha$-helix versus ``other'' ($\beta$-sheets and coils). Given an
amino acid sequence, the Forward-Backward algorithm \parencite{jurafsky} computes the exact
per-position posterior of each state --- our ground truth.

We then train a neural posterior estimator with \textit{BayesFlow} \parencite{bayesflow}
to approximate these posteriors from amino acid sequences alone, without access to the HMM
parameters, comparing two designs for the conditioning summary network: a staged design
that pretrains and freezes it, and a joint,
end-to-end design that lets BayesFlow's own training signal shape it directly
(Section~\ref{sec:joint}). The models are validated on held-out simulated sequences
and on a real protein, human insulin (PDB: 1A7F) \parencite{insulin}.

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the workflow: an HMM simulator produces
synthetic sequences with exact Forward--Backward posteriors, a BiLSTM--CouplingFlow
architecture amortises this posterior as a function of the sequence alone, and the trained
model is validated against the experimental annotation of a real protein.

\section{Data}

The data layer serves two purposes: producing an unlimited supply of simulated
amino-acid sequences with exact per-residue inference targets, and converting
human insulin 1A7F into the same sequence representation with a
compatible two-state validation target.

\subsection{Canonical representation}

Every amino acid is encoded as an integer between 0 and 19 using the canonical order
\begin{equation}
(\mathrm{A,R,N,D,C,E,Q,G,H,I,L,K,M,F,P,S,T,W,Y,V}).
\end{equation}
Thus, alanine would be coded 0, glycine 7, and valine 19. The encoding and decoding functions, the labels, and the probability arrays are all defined just once in \texttt{encoding.py}. The convention for the hidden states is $0=\alpha$-helix and $1=\text{other}$. The probability arrays will be verified when imported to sum to one.

\subsection{Simulated training data}

The simulator draws a state path $s_{1:L}$ from the two-state HMM of
Section~\ref{sec:stat}, then emits one amino acid per position from the
state's emission table, yielding the aligned sequences $s_{1:L}$ and
$x_{1:L}$.

All randomness comes from a NumPy \texttt{Generator}, so a fixed seed
reproduces everything exactly. For a
batch of $n$ examples, lengths are sampled independently from the discrete uniform
distribution
\begin{equation}
L_i \sim \mathcal{U}\{10,11,\ldots,60\}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
\end{equation}
covering the short insulin chains while retaining longer sequences with
several transitions. The training script can request as many examples as
needed; the final configuration uses 2000 training and 400 validation sequences,
plus independent held-out sequences.

The sampled state path is retained for diagnostics even though it is not
the training target.

\subsection{Exact targets and dataset handoff}

For every simulated observation $x_{1:L}$, exact HMM inference produces
\begin{equation}
y_t = P(S_t=0\mid X_{1:L}=x_{1:L}), \qquad t=1,\ldots,L,
\end{equation}
where state 0 denotes $\alpha$-helix; each training item is the integer sequence,
its per-position $\alpha$ posterior (a probability in $[0,1]$, not a hard
label), and the sampled state path.

Since different sequences have different lengths, they are put together in a single row and the vector of lengths is created along with it, eliminating any padding. The \texttt{.npz}file contains sequences, posteriors, states, and lengths, while the sum of lengths recreates the example without the need for object arrays or pickling. Table ~\ref{tab:data-interfaces} lists the main interfaces.

\section{Statistical model}
\label{sec:stat}

\subsection{Two-state Hidden Markov Model}

Define $S_t\in\{0,1\}$ to be the state of the secondary structure at position $t$, where $S_t=0$ represents an $\alpha$-helix and $S_t=1$ represents ``other'' ($\beta$-sheets and coils). Let $X_t\in\{0,\ldots,19\}$ represent the amino acid at that position. The model assumes a first-order Markov process with observations conditionally independent given the current state:

\begin{equation}
P(s_{1:L},x_{1:L})
= P(s_1)P(x_1\mid s_1)
  \prod_{t=2}^{L} P(s_t\mid s_{t-1})P(x_t\mid s_t).
\label{eq:hmm-joint}
\end{equation}

The initial distribution is deterministic:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\pi}
=\begin{bmatrix}P(S_1=0)&P(S_1=1)\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:start}
\end{equation}
Every simulated sequence therefore starts in the other state. State transitions follow
the row-stochastic matrix
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}=
\begin{bmatrix}
P(0\mid0)&P(1\mid0)\\
P(0\mid1)&P(1\mid1)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.90&0.10\\
0.05&0.95
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:transition}
\end{equation}
Thus $\alpha$-helix runs last on average $10$ residues, other runs $20$,
and the stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\mu}=(1/3,2/3)$ describes the
long-run state proportions. It is not the initial-state prior, which is
deterministic (Equation~\ref{eq:start}). Because the transition and emission
matrices are fixed, no prior is placed over the model parameters.

The state-conditional emission probabilities are the fixed empirical values in
Table~\ref{tab:emissions} (each row a categorical distribution over the canonical
order, summing to one).

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{Emission probabilities (\%) for the two HMM states.}
    \label{tab:emissions}
    \footnotesize
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.4pt}
    \begin{tabular}{l|rrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrrr}
        \hline
        State & A & R & N & D & C & E & Q & G & H & I & L & K & M & F & P & S & T & W & Y & V \\
        \hline
        $\alpha$ & 12 & 6 & 3 & 5 & 1 & 9 & 5 & 4 & 2 & 7 & 12 & 6 & 3 & 4 & 2 & 5 & 4 & 1 & 3 & 6 \\
        other & 6 & 5 & 5 & 6 & 2 & 5 & 3 & 9 & 3 & 5 & 8 & 6 & 2 & 4 & 6 & 7 & 6 & 1 & 4 & 7 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The emission table is intentionally simple: alanine and leucine favour
$\alpha$, glycine and proline ``other'', but the distributions overlap
substantially, so individual residues are not decisive; inference must combine
evidence across the full sequence.

A sequence of length $L$ is generated by drawing $S_1=1$
(Equation~\ref{eq:start}), then $S_t\sim\operatorname{Categorical}
(\mathbf{A}_{S_{t-1},:})$ for $t=2,\ldots,L$, and emitting
$X_t\sim\operatorname{Categorical}(\mathbf{B}_{S_t,:})$ at every position
($\mathbf{B}$ is Table~\ref{tab:emissions} divided by 100). This yields both
the observation $x_{1:L}$ and its latent path $s_{1:L}$; the neural
approximator never sees the path and is trained on the exact posterior
marginals from the observation alone.

\subsection{Forward-Backward inference}

Because all HMM parameters are known, the posterior probability of each state can be
computed exactly with the Forward-Backward algorithm \parencite{jurafsky}. Define the
forward quantity
\begin{equation}
f_t(j)=P(X_{1:t}=x_{1:t},S_t=j)
\end{equation}
and the backward quantity
\begin{equation}
b_t(i)=P(X_{t+1:L}=x_{t+1:L}\mid S_t=i).
\end{equation}
With emission probability $B_{j,x_t}=P(X_t=x_t\mid S_t=j)$, their recursions are
\begin{align}
f_1(j) &= \pi_j B_{j,x_1}, \\
f_t(j) &= B_{j,x_t}\sum_{i=0}^{1} f_{t-1}(i)A_{ij},
          &&t=2,\ldots,L, \\
b_L(i) &= 1, \\
b_t(i) &= \sum_{j=0}^{1}A_{ij}B_{j,x_{t+1}}b_{t+1}(j),
          &&t=L-1,\ldots,1.
\end{align}
The smoothed posterior marginal is then
\begin{equation}
\gamma_t(j)
=P(S_t=j\mid x_{1:L})
=\frac{f_t(j)b_t(j)}{\sum_{k=0}^{1}f_t(k)b_t(k)}.
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
The training target is the $\alpha$ column, $y_t=\gamma_t(0)$.

The code uses \texttt{hmmlearn.CategoricalHMM} \parencite{hmmlearn} with standard values for the constants \texttt{startprob\_}, \texttt{transmat\_}, and \texttt{emissionprob\_} (alphabet size 20). Fitting is not required because that would change the values of the parameters. Inference is done with \texttt{predict\_proba} on batches of sequences, with column 0 being the target.

\subsection{Verification}

A two-residue observation checks the transition orientation and emission
indexing. For $x=(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{A})$ (indices $(7,0)$), the
recursions give $f_2(0)=0.09\times0.05\times0.12=0.00054$
and $f_2(1)=0.09\times0.95\times0.06=0.00513$, hence
$P(S_2{=}0\mid\mathrm{G,A})=0.0952$, while
$P(S_1{=}0\mid\mathrm{G,A})=0$ due to the deterministic start; the library
output matches an independent NumPy implementation, as do six further short
sequences. On 2000 length-500 sequences (seed 12345) the
maximum empirical deviation from the specified transition and emission
probabilities was 0.0004 and 0.0007, below the Monte Carlo threshold of 0.01;
verification on 200 sequences confirmed posteriors in $[0,1]$, probabilities
summing to one, and zero $\alpha$ posterior at position 0. Fixed seeds reproduced
lengths, observations, targets, and latent paths exactly; save/load round-trips
were lossless, and all 45 automated tests for Sections~2--3 passed.

\subsection{Interpretation and limitations}

Equation (\ref{eq:gamma}) is now a deterministic function of the sequence with the HMM parameters remaining constant. In this way, the network amortizes the actual Forward-Backward calculation and not the parameter posterior (which is one reason why the following calibration tests are performed). The Hidden Markov Model is intentionally simple: There is only one state for the $\beta$-sheet and coil, there is independence from position in the emissions, and the deterministic start
forces an exact-zero $\alpha$ posterior at position 0.

\section{Approximator}
\label{sec:approx}

\subsection{Architecture overview}

We use BayesFlow \parencite{bayesflow} to train a neural posterior estimator. The
architecture consists of two components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Summary network:} A bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) that processes the
    variable-length amino acid sequence into a fixed-size summary vector; we use a
    BiLSTM rather than a permutation-invariant network because residue order matters
    for secondary structure prediction.
    \item \textbf{Inference network:} A \texttt{CouplingFlow} normalizing flow
    \parencite{couplingflow} that learns the conditional density of the per-position
    $\alpha$-helix posteriors given the sequence summary.
\end{enumerate}

The BiLSTM architecture is
\begin{multline*}
\text{Embedding}(21\to 32) \to \text{BiLSTM}(\dim=64,\ \text{2 layers},\ \text{dropout }0.2) \\
\to \text{masked mean pooling} \to \text{Linear+ReLU} \to \text{summary}(\dim=64).
\end{multline*}
A separate linear \texttt{position\_head} produces per-position logits for
direct point prediction of $P(\alpha)$; padding token 20 marks variable-length
sequences, \texttt{pack\_\allowbreak padded\_\allowbreak sequence} skips
padded positions, and the masked binary cross-entropy loss averages over real
positions only. The CouplingFlow uses affine coupling transforms with actnorm
and two-layer hidden subnets of width $128$; its depth comes from
hyperparameter search (Section~\ref{sec:training}).

\subsection{Two-stage design}

Training proceeds in two stages, reflecting the fact that the summary network is not
differentiated through BayesFlow's flow loss:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 — BiLSTM pretraining:} The summary network is trained
    on the masked BCE objective against Forward-Backward $\alpha$-helix
    posteriors, with an auxiliary loss predicting each sequence's mean $\alpha$-helix
    fraction, so both heads are well-trained.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 — CouplingFlow training:} The BiLSTM is frozen and
    each sequence is condensed into a condition vector by concatenating the pooled
    summary (dim~64) with the per-position logits (padded to \texttt{max\_len}=60).
    The CouplingFlow (hidden widths~128, affine transform, actnorm; depth
    from the search in Section~\ref{sec:training})
    is trained via \texttt{BasicWorkflow.fit\_offline} on
    $\{\text{condition} \to P(\alpha \mid \text{sequence})\}$.
\end{enumerate}

Raw logits (not sigmoid probabilities) enter the condition because the
CouplingFlow's base distribution is an unconstrained Gaussian; a $[0,1]$-bounded
signal would destabilize training.

\subsection{Joint (end-to-end) alternative}
\label{sec:joint}

We also trained the alternative the two-stage design avoids: letting gradients
from the CouplingFlow's loss reach the BiLSTM directly, with no freezing step.
This works because BayesFlow runs on Keras 3's \texttt{torch} backend;
wrapping the BiLSTM in \texttt{keras.\allowbreak layers.\allowbreak TorchModuleWrapper} lets
\texttt{BasicWorkflow} call it as a real summary network every step instead
of consuming a precomputed condition. Everything else is identical, and
results are reported alongside
the frozen design throughout Sections~\ref{sec:training}--\ref{sec:inference}.


\section{Training and Diagnostics}
\label{sec:training}

\subsection{Two-stage design and data regime}

Training is offline: a fixed set of 2000 training and 400
validation sequences (length $10$--$60$, padded to $60$) is generated once
and reused across every epoch. The pipeline runs in two stages: the
summary network (Section~\ref{sec:approx}) is pretrained on the
Forward--Backward posteriors, then frozen as a feature extractor to
condition the coupling flow; all losses and metrics use real positions only.

\subsection{Stage 1: summary-network pretraining}

The network minimises a masked binary cross-entropy against the soft
Forward--Backward targets (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay
$10^{-4}$, batch size $64$, gradient clipping at norm $1.0$,
\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau}, up to $60$ epochs, early stopping with patience
$8$). Its output forms a $124$-dimensional
condition: a $64$-dimensional pooled summary concatenated with the $60$
per-position logits. Conditioning on the pooled summary
alone discarded positional information and underperformed a sequence-blind
baseline; concatenating the per-position logits fixed that.

\subsection{Stage 2: coupling-flow training and model selection}

With the summary network frozen, the flow is trained with AdamW (learning
rate $5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$), batch size $64$, for $100$
epochs. The flow's objective
is a negative log-density and unbounded below, and roughly $44\%$ of every
padded target is exactly zero (padding plus the deterministic start at
position~$0$), so a continuous flow can drive the loss down just by
overfitting the padding. Configurations are instead ranked by masked mean
absolute error (MAE) on real positions. A
search over optimiser, learning rate, depth, and weight decay (six
configurations) selected a coupling-flow depth of $10$
(Figure~\ref{fig:training}).

\subsection{Convergence}

The Stage-1 loss plateaus around $0.46$ (Figure~\ref{fig:training}) rather
than going to zero, which is expected: for soft probability targets
cross-entropy is bounded below by their mean entropy. That
floor is $0.4621$ on the validation set and the network reaches $0.4623$ --- a
gap of only $0.0003$, essentially the theoretical minimum, with no sign of
overfitting.

\subsection{Joint training: configuration and cost}

The joint model reuses the same architectures, but
\texttt{fit\_\allowbreak offline} manages a single optimizer for the whole
model (one AdamW-with-cosine-warmup schedule) rather than the staged design's two
independently-tuned ones. Its hyperparameter search was also trimmed, to
four configurations, since joint training pays the
BiLSTM's forward/backward cost on every step of every trial instead of
once. The search picked learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$,
depth~$6$; depth $10$ scored worse here, so each design ends up compared
at its own best depth rather than a matched one. On this
(CPU-only) machine the final
$100$-epoch retrain took $33.5$ minutes, versus $\sim17$ minutes for the
whole $4$-configuration search.

\section{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diag}

All diagnostics are run on a holdout sample of $200$ simulated
sequences ($6{,}764$ actual positions) with each training scheme of
Section~\ref{sec:training}. Since the Forward--Backward
posterior is a deterministic mapping from the sequence, there is no classical
uncertainty about the parameters, and these diagnostics test whether the
dispersion in the flow correctly captures the residual error. Since the HMM
parameters do not vary and the performance of the neural posterior estimator is
compared against exact analytical results, posterior predictive checks (PPCs)
are not applicable.

\subsection{Simulation-Based Calibration (SBC)}

Standard SBC, following \textcite{sbc}, works like this: you draw parameters
from the prior, simulate new data, and then check whether the posterior ranks
look uniform. Our setup is a bit different: the HMM parameters are fixed. So we
tweak SBC --- for each position we rank where the true Forward--Backward value
falls among the BayesFlow posterior samples, and check whether those ranks
spread out evenly. If learning is perfect, the true value looks just like
another draw from the learned posterior, so you would expect the rank to be
uniform from $0$ to $200$.

Figure~\ref{fig:sbc} shows rank histograms and PIT ECDFs for both designs. The
frozen design gives a mean rank of $0.528$ ($44.2\%$ of ranks below $0.5$) and
a KS distance of $0.110$; the joint design has mean rank $0.519$ ($45.0\%$
below $0.5$) and KS $=0.132$. Both are close to uniform, with two quirks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spike in the first rank bin.} About $3\%$ of positions have
    exact-zero targets --- the deterministic start at position~$0$. A continuous
    flow cannot represent a point mass: it smears a bit of density around zero,
    so every one of the $200$ samples overshoots the truth and the rank ends up
    at $0$. That is not a real calibration problem --- just an artefact.
    \item \textbf{Mild central hump.} Ranks tend to pile up around the centre,
    which points to a mildly conservative (overdispersed) posterior.
\end{itemize}

Neither design is U-shaped (no sign of overconfident posteriors) and there is no
big directional bias. The joint design fits the posterior markedly better
point-wise (Section~\ref{sec:inference}) but pays slightly in rank uniformity
(KS $0.132$ vs.\ $0.110$), which just means its posteriors are a little
tighter.

\subsection{Z-score analysis and credible interval coverage}

Here is how the per-position $z$-scores work: for each position $t$, the
$z$-score is $z_t = (P_t^{\mathrm{true}} - \hat{\mu}_t)/\hat{\sigma}_t$,
where $\hat{\mu}_t$ is the posterior mean and $\hat{\sigma}_t$ is the
posterior standard deviation. If the posterior is well calibrated, these
$z$-scores should look like a standard normal distribution --- centered at $0$
with a spread of $1$.

Figure~\ref{fig:zscore} drops out positions that are exact zeros, which removes
$200$ of $6{,}764$ positions. Focusing on the frozen design, $63$ of $6{,}564$
positions (just about $1\%$) end up with off-the-charts $z$-scores (some as
extreme as $|z|\approx3137$), so they are left out of the plot. Most of these
outliers --- about $83\%$ --- show up near the protein's C-terminus (normalized
position $0.85$ or higher), which is where the posterior mean really shrinks
toward zero, even when the actual value is big. The remaining $z$-scores have
mean $0.129$ and standard deviation $0.709$. The joint design comes out much
cleaner: only $18$ extreme positions are left (median position $0.83$), the
biggest $|z|$ is $10.2$, and the mean and standard deviation are $0.089$ and
$0.691$, respectively. Basically, end-to-end training in the joint design helps
iron out that C-terminal issue.

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

\subsection{Synthetic held-out evaluation}

Let us look at Table~\ref{tab:synthetic} --- it shows how the models did on
$200$ simulated test sequences, covering $6{,}764$ residue positions. For
ground truth, we used the exact Forward--Backward posterior. BayesFlow
predicts the median of its posterior, while the BiLSTM gives a value straight
from its sigmoid output.

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{Performance on held-out simulated data (frozen vs.\ joint design).}
    \label{tab:synthetic}
    \begin{tabular}{l|c|c|c}
        \hline
        Model & Design & MAE & $r$ \\
        \hline
        BayesFlow (median) & Frozen & 0.057 & 0.778 \\
        BayesFlow (median) & Joint & \textbf{0.016} & \textbf{0.987} \\
        BiLSTM (point) & Frozen & \textbf{0.0035} & \textbf{0.9997} \\
        BiLSTM (point) & Joint & 0.300 & $-0.535$ \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The frozen BiLSTM nails it: it basically matches the Forward--Backward
posteriors, with a correlation of $0.9997$ and a mean absolute error (MAE) of
just $0.0035$. That tells us a neural network can essentially learn the
HMM's deterministic mapping.

Training BayesFlow end-to-end helps a lot: its median posterior drops the
MAE to $0.016$ and boosts the correlation to $0.987$ --- much better than the
frozen design's $0.057$ MAE and $0.778$ correlation. Why? Because end-to-end
training teaches the flow exactly what it needs from the encoder, not just
whatever comes from a point-prediction objective.

The joint BiLSTM is not much good as a point predictor (correlation is
negative: $-0.535$), but that is no surprise: it was never trained
specifically for the binary cross-entropy loss --- it is just extracting
features. So you really should not treat its outputs as probabilities. The
only setting where the BiLSTM baseline means anything is in the frozen
design.

Chain B, which has $34.5\%$ helix content, fares well with both models:
their predictions line up with the real annotation (correlation around
$0.85$), and the predicted helix fraction sits near the HMM's long-term
average (around $33\%$). In other words, the prior lines up with the
reality here, so both models do fine.

Chain A is a different story. It has $61.9\%$ helix, but both models
struggle (correlation only about $0.4$). Why? The stationary prior still
expects about $33\%$ helix, pulling predictions away from what is true.
With just $21$ residues, the emission tables cannot really fight that
prior. Even if you run Forward--Backward directly on insulin, chain~A still
gets a low correlation (about $0.40$). So the trouble comes from the
two-state HMM itself, not from the neural approximator.

The most telling comparison is when we look at both designs side by side on
insulin. The jointly trained model nails the Forward--Backward posterior on
synthetic data (correlation almost perfect at $0.987$), but for real
insulin nothing changes --- the correlations stay pretty much the same
(chain~A: $0.391$ vs.\ $0.428$; chain~B: $0.854$ vs.\ $0.851$). So even
with a nearly perfect estimator, the model cannot go beyond the limitations
set by the simulator. For chain~A, that is clear evidence of a hard cap ---
a \emph{model ceiling} --- that extra training cannot fix. No matter what
you do, there will always be a gap between the two chains.


\section{Discussion \& Conclusion}

\subsection{What BayesFlow contributes}

In the frozen design the BiLSTM point predictor is more accurate on synthetic
data than the BayesFlow posterior median ($r = 0.9997$ vs.\ $0.778$): the
Forward--Backward target is a deterministic function of the input, so a
direct network learns this map more efficiently than a flow.
Joint training lets the summary network adapt to the flow's objective, and the
posterior median reaches $r = 0.987$, nearly closing that gap.

BayesFlow nevertheless provides what a point predictor cannot: posterior
samples with credible intervals (Figure~\ref{fig:contraction}), trustworthy
calibration (both designs over-cover at
every nominal level, Table~\ref{tab:coverage}), and an amortized framework that
generalizes to genuine parameter-uncertainty settings.

The insulin results are the strongest argument for the pipeline: despite a
near-perfect synthetic posterior ($r=0.987$), the real-protein correlations
are unchanged (chain~A $\approx 0.4$, chain~B $\approx 0.85$) --- the
posterior estimator is not the limiting factor, the two-state HMM is.

\subsection{Model limitations}

The two-state HMM with fixed emission tables is an oversimplified model of
protein secondary structure: the stationary $\alpha$-helix prior of $\tfrac{1}{3}$
biases predictions and the emission tables cannot overcome it
on short sequences, as most evident on insulin chain~A, where the model fails
whether inference is exact or approximate.

\subsection{Potential improvements}

The most impactful improvement would be a richer simulator --- a separate
$\beta$-sheet state, position-dependent emissions, or a context-aware state
process --- expected to close part of the chain~A gap. A transformer encoder
could improve the summary network, online
simulation (fresh sequences per batch) would remove overfitting to the fixed
dataset, and validating on a panel of
diverse proteins would make the chain~A/chain~B contrast systematic.

\subsection{Reflection on own learnings}

This project made simulation-based inference concrete: a network can answer
inference questions about a simulator without touching its internals, at a
price paid in calibration rather than in fit. The staged-vs.-joint comparison
showed that training a summary network is a modelling choice with measurable
consequences, and hand-implementing the diagnostics taught us what
``calibrated'' means. The
insulin result was most instructive: a
perfectly trained inference engine cannot outperform its simulator.

\subsection{Conclusion}

We implemented a full simulation-based inference pipeline for protein
secondary structure --- HMM simulation, Forward-Backward ground truth, BiLSTM
summary network, BayesFlow CouplingFlow training, and calibration
diagnostics (adapted SBC, z-score analysis, credible interval coverage).
Validation on synthetic and real data reveals the strengths of the neural
approximator (faithful learning of the target, well-behaved uncertainty on
chain~B) and the fundamental limitations of the two-state HMM as a generative model of
real proteins.
